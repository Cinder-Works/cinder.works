How We Built a Custom 3D Printed Map Coaster From Scratch

There's a moment in every product's life where it goes from "what if" to "oh, that's actually going to work." For our 3D printed map coaster, that moment was watching OpenSCAD render 247 buildings, 83 roads, and 6 parks from a real neighborhood — clipped into a perfect 90mm circle — and thinking: I can hold that.

This is the full story of how we built it. The pipeline, the geometry problems, the design decisions, the unit economics. Everything.

The Idea: Your Neighborhood, in Your Hand

Everybody has a place that matters to them. The street they grew up on. The campus where they pulled all-nighters. The block where they got engaged. The hospital where their kid was born.

What if you could hold it?

Not a flat print. Not a laser-etched slab. A real, textured, multicolor 3D printed map coaster — roads raised in white, buildings in gray, parks in green, water in blue, all sitting on a clean black base. Pick it up and you'd start pointing at streets. That's the coffee shop. That's the park where we walk the dog.

That was the pitch. And when we searched Etsy for "custom map coaster," we found plenty of engraved wooden ones, a few laser-cut acrylic versions, and zero — zero — multicolor 3D printed map coasters.

A genuine market gap. The kind that makes you lean forward and start typing.

The Problem Nobody Else Has Solved

Here's why the gap exists: you can't pre-design inventory for this product. Every address on Earth has a different street layout. A custom map coaster of downtown Manhattan looks nothing like one of suburban Phoenix, which looks nothing like a small coastal town in Portugal.

An infinite SKU space. No human designer can keep up with that.

But a parametric pipeline can.

The idea was simple in theory, brutal in execution: customer types an address, code fetches real map data, generates a unique 3D model, and outputs print-ready files. Fully automated. Every single 3D printed map coaster geometrically unique because every location on Earth is unique.

Simple, right? Just geocoding, geographic data processing, coordinate projection, polygon clipping, computational geometry, and multi-body 3D model generation. All stitched together in one script.

Easy Monday.

Building the Pipeline

Step 1: From Address to Coordinates

The pipeline starts with a street address. We hit the Nominatim API — the geocoding service behind OpenStreetMap — and get back latitude and longitude. Free, open, covers the entire planet. A customer in Tokyo and a customer in Toronto go through the exact same door.

Step 2: Pulling Real Map Data

With coordinates in hand, we query the Overpass API — the powerhouse behind OpenStreetMap data extraction. One query pulls every road, building, park, and water feature within a 400-meter radius of the target location.

The query looks deceptively clean:

way["highway"](around:400, lat, lon);
way["building"](around:400, lat, lon);
way["leisure"="park"](around:400, lat, lon);
way["natural"="water"](around:400, lat, lon);

Four lines. What comes back is a flood of real cartographic data — node coordinates, way geometries, tag metadata. Every road classified by type (primary, residential, service). Every building as a polygon. Every park boundary. The same source that powers countless mapping applications worldwide.

Step 3: The Hard Part — Geometry Processing

This is where the project nearly stalled.

Raw OpenStreetMap data is in WGS84 coordinates — latitude and longitude on a sphere. Our coaster is a flat 90mm disc. Bridging that gap means:

1. Coordinate projection. Convert lat/lon to local millimeters, correcting for the cosine of latitude (because a degree of longitude is narrower near the poles than at the equator). Get this wrong and your map is stretched like taffy.

2. Road buffering. OSM stores roads as centerlines — infinitely thin lines with no width. We buffer each road into a polygon based on its classification. Major roads get 1.8mm width. Residential streets get 1.2mm. Footpaths get 0.84mm. This is where the map starts to feel like a real place — the highway is bolder than the side street.

3. Circular clipping. Everything gets clipped to a circle. Not a square crop — a proper geometric intersection against a 90mm circular boundary using Shapely. Roads that cross the edge get cleanly sliced. Buildings on the perimeter become partial shapes. Computationally expensive. Looks right.

4. Polygon merging. Hundreds of individual road segments get merged into unified geometries via unary_union. This matters for print quality — fewer polygons mean cleaner STL files and faster OpenSCAD rendering.

Step 4: Four Files, Four Colors

The pipeline outputs four separate OpenSCAD files, one per color layer:

  • base.scad — Black disc. 2mm tall. The foundation everything sits on.
  • roads.scad — White. 3mm tall (2mm base height + 1mm relief). Every road in the neighborhood, buffered to width.
  • buildings.scad — Gray/blue. 3.5mm tall (2mm + 1.5mm relief). The tallest layer — buildings pop.
  • parks.scad — Green. 2.8mm tall (2mm + 0.8mm relief). A subtle layer filling in the green spaces.

Each file renders to STL via OpenSCAD. Four STLs load into the slicer as a multi-color project. Four colors load into the AMS on our Bambu Lab X1C.

Here's the clever bit: the base height of 2mm means the bottom of every layer is solid black. The printer lays down black filament for the first 2mm across the entire coaster with zero color changes. The multicolor magic only happens in the top millimeter or two. This cuts print time and material waste significantly — every filament switch means purging the old color and priming the new one, and we've concentrated all the color work at the top to minimize that.

The Design Decisions That Mattered

Bold Rim, Clean Edge

Early prototypes had map data running right to the edge of the disc. It looked messy — roads getting sliced at arbitrary angles, half-buildings dangling off the perimeter.

The fix was a 0.5mm inset margin — clip the map data slightly inside the disc edge, leaving a clean black rim around the entire coaster. It frames the map like a picture frame and gives the whole piece a finished, intentional look. Small detail. Enormous visual impact.

Road Width Hierarchy

One of the earliest realizations: road width is the map's visual hierarchy. If every road is the same width, the coaster looks like a plate of spaghetti. But when the highway is visibly wider than the side street, your eye naturally reads the structure of the neighborhood. You see the main arteries first, then the residential grid, then the footpaths. It works exactly like a real map — because it is one.

Relief Height as Storytelling

Buildings are the tallest layer at 1.5mm of relief. Roads are 1mm. Parks are 0.8mm. This isn't arbitrary — it's a visual and tactile hierarchy. When you run your finger across the coaster, you feel the buildings standing above the streets. The parks are gentle depressions between structures. The map has topography even though the underlying data is flat. That's the part that makes people pick it up and not put it down.

What We Learned (The Hard Way)

OpenStreetMap data quality varies wildly. Downtown Manhattan has exquisite building data — nearly every structure outlined. Rural Montana might have roads and nothing else. We built in a quality check: if a location returns fewer than a threshold of features, we flag it and reach out to the customer before printing. "Your area is beautiful but sparse — want us to widen the radius to capture more detail?"

Polygon validity is not guaranteed. OSM building polygons can be self-intersecting, which makes Shapely throw a fit. The fix: poly.buffer(0) — a zero-width buffer that forces the geometry engine to clean up invalid polygons. Ugly hack. Works perfectly.

OpenSCAD has performance limits. Dense urban areas generate thousands of polygons. Dumping them all raw into OpenSCAD would take forever to render. The unary_union merge step in Shapely reduces polygon count dramatically before it hits OpenSCAD, keeping render times sane.

Color changes are expensive. Every filament switch means purged material and recalibration. The 2mm all-black base layer isn't just an aesthetic choice — it's a direct cost optimization. Less switching = less waste = lower material cost per coaster.

The Numbers

Let's talk business, because the economics here are genuinely exciting.

Material cost per coaster: approximately $0.29. That's PLA filament for a 90mm disc, 3.5mm tall. Less than thirty cents.

Etsy listing price: $19.99 with free shipping baked in (Etsy's search algorithm rewards free shipping listings).

After Etsy fees (~30%) and USPS First Class shipping (~$3.50): roughly $10.50 profit per custom map coaster.

That's a 53% net margin on a fully custom, made-to-order product. No inventory risk. No warehouse. No minimum order quantity. One printer, one person, one script.

At capacity — around 8-10 coasters per day on a single Bambu Lab X1C — that's $84-105 daily profit from one product line. Not retirement money. But for a product that didn't exist two weeks ago, the trajectory matters more than the snapshot.

The Moat

Here's why this product matters beyond the unit economics.

Nobody else can do this at scale. Not because the technology is secret — OpenStreetMap is open data, OpenSCAD is open source, Bambu Lab printers are consumer hardware. But the integration is the moat. Turning an address into a print-ready multicolor 3D model in under a minute requires a pipeline that handles geocoding, geographic data extraction, coordinate projection, computational geometry, polygon optimization, and multi-body STL generation. Every step has edge cases. Every edge case required a specific solution.

You could replicate any individual step. Replicating the whole chain — tested against addresses from Phoenix to London to Tokyo, with quality controls and production workflow around it — that's the thing that takes time. Time we've already spent.

And every coaster we print makes the pipeline smarter. Every edge case we handle makes the next order smoother. The compound advantage builds with volume.

That's the kind of product worth building.
